# DGBN Production Brief — Côte d’Ivoire Gold Refinery

## Editorial headline
**Côte d’Ivoire’s Next Big Bet: From Cocoa Powerhouse to Gold Refining Hub**

## Standfirst
Côte d’Ivoire plans to start operations at its first national gold-refining unit in Abidjan by the end of the first half of 2027. The refinery is designed to process up to 100 tonnes of gold annually, while the government says it wants the country to become Africa’s largest gold producer by 2035.

## Rewritten story
Côte d’Ivoire built its global agricultural reputation on cocoa. Now the West African country is trying to capture a much bigger share of another commodity value chain: gold.

On 16 September 2026, the Ivorian government announced that a new gold-refining unit being developed through the Société Ivoirienne de Mines et de Métaux Précieux, or SIMEP, is expected to begin operating by the end of the first half of 2027.

The Abidjan refinery is designed to process up to 100 tonnes of gold a year. That figure is refining capacity, not current national mine output. Côte d’Ivoire produced 59.33 tonnes of gold in 2025, according to figures reported by Reuters in June, and officials projected output of about 62 tonnes for 2026.

The government’s longer-term target is much more ambitious: by 2035 it says it wants Côte d’Ivoire to become Africa’s largest gold producer.

But the strategy extends beyond producing more metal. SIMEP is intended to give national actors a position across the gold chain — from semi-industrial mining and a national purchasing desk to refining, international marketing and the creation of a state gold stock.

The government says Ivorian capital will account for 70 percent of SIMEP. The state and state mining company SODEMI together hold 49 percent. StoneX is expected to support the international marketing of refined gold.

The national buying desk is also intended to aggregate gold from artisanal and small-scale mining, strengthen traceability and pull more of the sector into formal channels.

That makes the refinery significant for a reason beyond its headline capacity. Côte d’Ivoire is trying to move from being mainly an extractor of gold to controlling more of what happens after extraction: buying, refining, storing and selling.

The 2035 goal is an ambition rather than an achieved ranking, and reaching it will depend on new mines, expansion projects, investment, governance and execution. But the direction of policy is clear: Côte d’Ivoire wants more of the economic value created by its mineral resources to remain inside the country.

For a nation already known worldwide as a cocoa powerhouse, gold is becoming a major test of whether resource-producing African economies can move further up the value chain.

The government says the country’s first national gold-refining unit, in Abidjan, is expected to begin operations by the end of the first half of 2027. The refinery, developed through SIMEP, is designed to process up to one hundred tonnes of gold a year.

But that number needs context. One hundred tonnes is refining capacity, not Côte d’Ivoire’s current mine output. The country produced 59.33 tonnes of gold in 2025, and officials projected about 62 tonnes for 2026.

The ambition is much larger. By 2035, the government says it wants Côte d’Ivoire to become Africa’s largest gold producer.

And this project is about more than one refinery. SIMEP is intended to connect semi-industrial mining, a national gold-buying desk, processing, international marketing and the creation of a state gold stock. The government says national investors will hold seventy percent of the company, with the state and SODEMI together holding forty-nine percent.

StoneX is expected to support international marketing, while the buying system is also intended to improve traceability and bring more artisanal and small-scale gold into formal channels.

That may be the bigger story. Côte d’Ivoire is not only trying to extract more gold. It is trying to control more of the value chain — from the mine, to the refinery, to the global market.

For a country known worldwide for cocoa, gold could become the next major test of how much value can be processed and retained at home.
